# Scanning Decides Whether the Client Stays

The client's Sept 14, 2026 walkthrough asks for one thing: **CardUploader's ungraded-card seller workflow, rebuilt for Pokémon first and One Piece second**. In that workflow a seller uploads one or two images per card and gets every card identified automatically, checks the matches side by side, prices against the TCGplayer market with a $0.99 floor and a reminder of last time's price, merges duplicates and bulk-edits the batch, checks live eBay listings through links carrying the client's affiliate tag, and exports an eBay CSV with a shortcut to eBay's upload page. The client treats this as a go/no-go at **$7–10 a month** and will move on to real-estate projects if the core can't be delivered. Most of the workflow now exists in code. But the step the client called "key" still fails on the live site, which runs a **607-card demo catalog with no One Piece**: 13 seconds into the recording, all **15 of the client's scans show as "Unmatched"** on our site, and minutes later CardUploader returns a match for every one. Beyond matching CardUploader, the platform still needs a measured accuracy test on the client's own scans, an identification pipeline that crops each card before matching instead of hashing whole photos, eBay's trading-card condition descriptors in its CSV and API listings, Stripe billing that meets auto-renewal law, Terms of Service, and working email. Three licensing risks threaten the "other ways to make money" meant to keep the price low: the client's eBay **Ambassador** account likely earns nothing on the app's generic eBay links, the whole catalog and price feed comes from an unlicensed mirror of TCGplayer's data, and the public sold-price archive likely breaches eBay's API license. The app's wording needs its own cleanup, covering two product names, at least 30 concepts with two to six labels each, developer jargon on customer screens, and about 30 overstated promises, several of which are legal risks rather than cosmetic problems.

## A whole-frame hash cannot carry a go/no-go decision

Production identifies photos with a perceptual hash, a compact fingerprint of an image's light-and-dark pattern. It computes two 64-bit hashes (a gradient hash and an average hash) from a tiny greyscale thumbnail of the **whole uploaded frame**, and again from a copy trimmed 8% at the edges. It then compares them one by one against a single reference image per catalog product ([imagehash.ts](../card-db-mvp/src/imagehash.ts#L85), [vision.ts](../card-db-mvp/src/app/vision.ts#L140)).

If the distance exceeds 18, the matcher returns nothing. The item falls back to its filename and lands in review with no visual candidates. A hit is converted back into text and searched again, so the alternatives a seller sees come from that text search rather than from look-alike images. The printing is never read from the pixels ([vision.ts](../card-db-mvp/src/app/vision.ts#L151), [identify.ts](../card-db-mvp/src/app/identify.ts#L412)). In single-card mode nothing crops, straightens or rotates the photo, and the multi-card detector only engages when it finds at least two cards ([app.ts](../card-db-mvp/src/render/app.ts#L1745)).

None of this has been tested on a real photo. The **500-of-500** result used catalog images. The "phone" test image is an upscaled catalog scan, and the binder pages are synthetic composites ([test image](../test-cards/phone/01.jpg), [binder generator](../test-cards/make-binder-pages.mjs)). A production run before the Sept 13 index builder returned all 500 cards as needing review, with 0% matched ([inventory](../cardindex-feature-inventory.md)). The plan itself calls hashing fragile to glare, tilt and cropping ([plan doc](../client-video-2026-09-14-asks-and-plan.md)).

The likely failures, roughly in order of impact, begin with a scanner-bed margin or tabletop background swamping a 64-bit hash of the whole frame. Next come rotated cards that produce unrelated hashes, near-ties among tens of thousands of products that share frame designs, and the visual answer lost in the round trip through text search. Pokémon finishes (holo, reverse holo, 1st Edition) share one catalog image, so a hash can never tell them apart ([catalog-import.ts](../card-db-mvp/src/app/catalog-import.ts#L19)). The one piece of good news is that the client's files come from a scanner, with even light and no perspective. That is the easiest real-world input, so many of the 15 could match once One Piece is loaded. Nobody has measured it.

Current systems use a pipeline rather than a single matcher. They find and flatten each card, retrieve the top candidates with an image embedding (a learned visual fingerprint), and apply known constraints such as the batch's set. They then read the printed code or number, break near-ties by comparing fine image detail, and send anything uncertain to a top-five picker.

The best public evaluation, TCG-AR (July 2026), identified cards among **20,360 Pokémon cards at 85.1% top-1 and 96.2% top-5** on real footage. Top-1 rose to **96.4%** when candidates were restricted to a known list, and three-quarters of its top-1 misses still had the right card in the top five ([TCG-AR](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.02090v1)). That restriction effect is why the optional "prioritize this set" control matters more than the client suggested. Thirteen of the 14 distinct cards in the demo batch came from one set ([06:56](file:///C:/Users/johnn/Downloads/2026-09-14%2020-47-22.mp4#t=416)), so limiting the match to that set is nearly free accuracy.

Hashing can still serve as a fast first pass, about 10 ms per scan in one Magic scanner, but only after the card is cropped and flattened, and with a 256-bit hash of the artwork ([ManaMesh](https://github.com/KarimLeexz/ManaMesh)). For One Piece, the printed code (OP11-117, EB02-005) narrows a card to its base printing and its parallels, which are separate catalog products with their own images ([Bounty](https://www.bountytcg.app/en/blog/one-piece-tcg-set-codes-explained)). For Pokémon finishes, the realistic answer is a one-tap finish chooser.

Rivals set a modest but real bar. One tester's rough estimates put rival apps at about **85–95% accuracy on the card itself** for sleeved phone photos of modern English Pokémon. That falls to **60–70% on parallels and reverse holos**, and to 20–30 points lower inside toploaders ([HobbyCardIndex](https://hobbycardindex.com/hubs/pokemon-card-scanners/)). CardUploader claims over 95% on standard English and Japanese cards ([CardUploader FAQ](https://carduploader.com/guides/faq)), and it avoids variant errors by not storing holo or reverse-holo versions at all ([teardown](../carduploader-competitive-research-report.md)). Our catalog does track variants, but that is an advantage only if the interface asks which finish a card has instead of quietly picking a default.

| Engine | Cost per card | Speed per card | Accuracy evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Current 64-bit whole-frame hash | about $0 | Not measured at full-catalog scale | 500 of 500 on catalog art only; never tested on a real photo |
| Ximilar API (Pokémon EN/JP/CN, One Piece) | about $0.007 on the $69 plan ([pricing](https://www.ximilar.com/pricing/)) | "Usually a second" | Vendor's own claim only |
| CardSight API (One Piece since July 2026) | $0.002–0.003 ([CardSight](https://cardsight.ai/for-developers)) | Under 300 ms claimed | Vendor's own "99.5% mAP" claim |
| Self-hosted embedding on Railway CPU | about $0, plus $15–20 a month of memory ([Railway](https://docs.railway.com/pricing/plans)) | 0.5–1.5 s estimated | Closest published result: 85.1% top-1, 96.2% top-5 on real Pokémon footage |

Both vendors already recognise both games, and either could plug into the app's existing recognition-provider hook, which today keeps only the vendor's top name and throws away its alternatives ([vision.ts](../card-db-mvp/src/app/vision.ts#L174)). But their fees clash with the client's price. At the client's pace of about 5,000 cards a month, fees of $0.002–0.007 per card would cost the platform **$10–35 a month for that one seller**, more than a $7 membership. A self-hosted tier costs almost nothing per card, but it needs weeks of work, and its accuracy is unknown until it is built.

The fastest route to the client's answer is to measure rather than build a model. Build a golden set of a few hundred of the client's own scans, starting with the 15 from the video, and label each with its TCGplayer product and finish. Within days, run it through the current hash on the full catalog, both vendors' free tiers, and CardUploader itself, and report top-1 and top-5 accuracy on both the card and the variant.

In parallel, several fixes raise accuracy whichever engine wins. Crop and flatten every photo and try both orientations, keep the visual candidate from start to finish and always show five, read the printed code, add a finish chooser, and move identification into a background job with a progress bar. A reasonable bar is **95% top-1 and 99% top-5 on the client's scanner images**. Set automatic acceptance where 99% of auto-accepted cards are correct, with separate thresholds for the card and its finish. Logging every confirmation from day one is what finally makes "learns over time" real, because those confirmed matches become the training data for a self-hosted model. The client has never stated an accuracy target and should be asked for one.

## Parity is only the floor in a $10 market governed by eBay

### Rivals already sell this workflow for about $10

The client's product competes with dedicated scan-to-list tools, and the entry price there is crowded. CardUploader charges **$9.99 a month for unlimited** processing ([CardUploader pricing](https://carduploader.com/pricing)). TCG Automate charges $10 for 500 scans and $40 for cross-listing, managed inventory and beta repricing ([TCG Automate](https://www.tcgautomate.com/pricing)). Card Dealer Pro charges $9 for 500 credits ([Card Dealer Pro](https://www.carddealerpro.com/)), CardLuma $10 for 1,000 scans ([CardLuma](https://www.cardluma.com/cardluma_vs_carddealerpro.html)), and Kronozio from $10 ([Kronozio](https://kronozio.com/pricing)).

At $7 the client would undercut every dedicated bulk-listing tool found and sit beside Collectr Pro's $7.99 ([Collectr](https://getcollectr.com/pro)). At $10 the product must match "unlimited" or look worse than CardUploader. Combined with the vendor fees above, that points to a capped $7 starter plan alongside a $10–15 unlimited tier. Annual discounts are an easy opening: they are standard among rivals but missing from CardUploader's public pricing page.

**eBay has already made the first step a commodity.** Its free Price Guide scans raw or graded Pokémon (English and Japanese) and One Piece cards, shows up to two years of eBay sales including accepted Best Offer prices, and can start a listing draft, with no usage limits ([eBay Price Guide](https://pages.ebay.com/price-guide/)). It reached all U.S. sellers in the app by June 2026 ([eBay Seller Center](https://www.ebay.com/sellercenter/news/2026-june/trading-card-price-tool)). A paid tool therefore wins only on bulk work: hundreds of cards per batch, scanner input, better listings (titles, item specifics, condition descriptors, policies), and a clean hand-off to Seller Hub or the API.

The 2026 minimum is essentially the client's ten stages, plus eBay's condition descriptors and checks before upload. Ranked by value to this client, the features that would set the product apart are sold-price evidence at the moment of pricing, depth in One Piece and Japanese cards, fast queue-free processing of scanner batches, validated listing through the eBay API, and live sync across selling platforms. Sellers' recurring complaints about existing tools match the client's go/no-go almost exactly: misses on promos, foils, parallels and non-English cards, prices that don't match eBay, usage caps, processing queues, and CSV condition errors ([TCGplayer app reviews](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/tcgplayer/id1247645833?see-all=reviews), [Collectr reviews](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/collectr-tcg-collector-app/id1603892248?see-all=reviews), [eBay Community](https://community.ebay.com/t5/Report-eBay-Technical-Issues/Error-for-Conditions-when-bulk-listing-trading-cards/td-p/35141503)).

CardUploader keeps adding features without raising its price. Since early September it has added three: an automatic sync with the Misprint marketplace that covers exactly the client's two games ([Misprint guide](https://carduploader.com/guides/automatic-misprint-inventory)), a cross-listing beta that shares one stock count between eBay and Mana Pool ([cross-listing guide](https://carduploader.com/guides/cross-listing)), and a documented storefront and buylist ([storefront guide](https://carduploader.com/guides/storefront-buylist)). All are promised inside the one plan ([roadmap video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qXXryu2JU9Q)). It has also removed its public sales lookup, whose page now returns 404 ([CardUploader /sales](https://carduploader.com/sales)). A CSV-only rival falls further behind each month, even though the client doesn't want orders yet. The removal reopens public sold prices as a way to stand out, but that is exactly where eBay's license is strictest, as the next subsection shows.

**The clearest opening is One Piece and Japanese cards.** TCGplayer has a Japanese Pokémon category but none for Japanese One Piece, so there is no TCGplayer market price feed for those cards ([TCGCSV categories](https://tcgcsv.com/tcgplayer/categories)). CardUploader's game menu in the recording does list a One Piece Japanese database, which corrects an earlier reading of its code that found only English ([03:12](file:///C:/Users/johnn/Downloads/2026-09-14%2020-47-22.mp4#t=192)). But nothing on screen shows where it prices those cards from. Specialist One Piece apps such as Haki track Japanese prints and parallels, yet none of the apps reviewed offers bulk eBay listing ([Haki](https://hakitcg.com/blog/best-one-piece-card-scanner-apps-2026)). Our importer already treats One Piece parallels as separate products with their own images, the right model for a game whose value is concentrated in parallels and alternate arts ([catalog-import.ts](../card-db-mvp/src/app/catalog-import.ts#L19)). Japanese Pokémon (TCGplayer's category 85) is not imported yet ([sync-tcgcsv.ts](../card-db-mvp/src/scripts/sync-tcgcsv.ts#L35)).

The client's growth plan of teaching, referrals and a Discord matches how this niche grows. CardUploader pays referrers 10% of each subscription every month and is launching a creator fund ([roadmap video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qXXryu2JU9Q)). TCG Automate pays a 50% commission up to $50 per referred user ([TCG Automate affiliate](https://www.tcgautomate.com/affiliate)), and Collectr reached 4 million users and eight-figure annual revenue without advertising ([BetaKit](https://betakit.com/how-collectr-bootstrapped-a-trading-card-hobby-into-an-eight-figure-business/)). At $7, a 10% monthly commission pays a referrer 70 cents a month, too little to motivate creators, so a one-time bounty is easier to fund.

One naming hazard also turned up. A live product called **Card Index**, at cardindex.com, already sells Pokémon and One Piece pricing and a Whatnot scanner extension updated Sept 7, 2026 ([cardindex.com](https://cardindex.com/), [Chrome Web Store](https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/card-index-%E2%80%94-whatnot-card/impoppkbagmiajilcfbnbepdohahjmff)). That collides with the app's internal name.

### Licensing, not code, threatens the low-price business model

eBay requires three things before an app goes into production. The app must subscribe to eBay's Marketplace Account Deletion notices, list a privacy-policy URL under its RuName (its registered redirect name), and comply with the API License Agreement, last revised June 24, 2025 ([deletion rules](https://developer.ebay.com/marketplace-account-deletion), [license](https://developer.ebay.com/join/api-license-agreement), [developer newsletter](https://developer.ebay.com/updates/newsletter/q2_2025)). The deletion endpoint is built and deployed. eBay only turns on a production keyset after that subscription, so the live listing results suggest it is in place; the developer portal should confirm it.

The license also imposes requirements the site doesn't yet meet. It requires a binding user agreement that holds users to eBay's terms and names eBay a third-party beneficiary, yet the site has **no Terms of Service** at all ([/terms](https://tradingcardloader.com/terms)). It requires industry-standard security, including encryption of stored data and two-factor sign-in for admins, yet eBay tokens and the eBay app secret are stored in plain text, and the owner console uses a password alone ([ebay-sell.ts](../card-db-mvp/src/app/ebay-sell.ts#L55)). And eBay notes that its Buy APIs, which include the Browse API behind the eBay Listed popup, need an additional license, which should be confirmed with eBay ([Buy API limits](https://developer.ebay.com/develop/api/buy/api_call_limits)).

Three built features conflict with the license. First, the license bars using eBay content to suggest or model prices, which covers the popup's "listed from $lo to $hi" summary and the sales lookup's average of the last three sales ([app.ts](../card-db-mvp/src/render/app.ts#L2051), [sales.ts](../card-db-mvp/src/sales.ts#L227)). The popup can stay as a plain listing display, which the license permits, without the summary. Second, eBay's picture policy bans stock photos for used items ([picture policy](https://www.ebay.com/help/policies/listing-policies/picture-policy?id=4370)), yet API listings attach catalog images to every card, and the public card page offers listings with "no scan needed" ([ebay-sell.ts](../card-db-mvp/src/app/ebay-sell.ts#L427), [pages.ts](../card-db-mvp/src/render/pages.ts#L481)).

Third, the public sold-price archive pulls in 730 days of a seller's paid orders the moment they connect. It links each row to the seller's own listing, can reveal accepted Best Offer prices that eBay itself hides, and averages them beside rows from other marketplaces ([soldharvest.ts](../card-db-mvp/src/app/soldharvest.ts#L56)). The license requires each seller's explicit advance consent before data visible only after sign-in goes public, and eBay's written permission before average prices are derived from its data. eBay retired its old Finding API in February 2025, and its sold-data replacement is closed to new users ([SoldComps](https://sold-comps.com/alternatives)), so there is no open route to market-wide sold data. The safe design keeps harvested sales private to each seller. That clashes with the market's top-ranked differentiator: sold-price evidence is valuable precisely because it is hard to get lawfully.

The affiliate plan faces two problems. A new eBay Partner Network (EPN) agreement, published Sept 24, 2026 and effective Sept 28 for existing partners, treats software applications and tool-generated links as methods needing EPN's prior written approval. EPN withholds all commission on traffic from unapproved applications ([EPN agreement](https://partnernetwork.ebay.com/page/network-agreement)). The agreement's Ambassador section pays only on curated listings sold while they are available in the Ambassador portal, and bars Ambassadors from any other EPN program without written permission. Tagging every eBay search, sold search and item link with the client's Ambassador campaign therefore likely earns nothing. The client would need a standard EPN account, with EPN's permission, plus approval for the app. Even then the sums are small: the client's own Ambassador portal estimated **$0.11 per sale** of a $1.49 card ([11:20](file:///C:/Users/johnn/Downloads/2026-09-14%2020-47-22.mp4#t=680)).

The disclosure wording already matches EPN's example, but its placement falls short. The sales lookup puts it below the results, and the popup says only "affiliate links", whereas EPN and FTC staff guidance want disclosures close to the links and in clear words ([EPN disclosure FAQ](https://partnernetwork.ebay.com/resources/affiliate-disclosure-faq), [FTC FAQ](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/ftcs-endorsement-guides-what-people-are-asking)). The owner's own clicks should not be tagged, since EPN treats self-clicks as invalid. TCGplayer's affiliate program runs on the Impact platform ([Lasso](https://getlasso.co/affiliate/tcgplayer/)), and the app only appends tracking parameters to TCGplayer links ([affiliate.ts](../card-db-mvp/src/affiliate.ts#L32)). That may not credit the client until it is checked against a real Impact tracking link.

The data the app is built on raises the gravest question. TCGplayer's API is closed to new developers ([TCGplayer docs](https://docs.tcgplayer.com/docs/getting-started)). Its API terms forbid getting its pricing from a third party that collected it through the API, and forbid combining its prices with other pricing data ([API terms](https://help.tcgplayer.com/hc/en-us/articles/360061115874-TCGplayer-API-Terms-Conditions)). Its site terms claim its pricing information and card databases as TCGplayer property and ban scraping ([TCGplayer terms](https://help.tcgplayer.com/hc/en-us/articles/205004918-Terms-of-Service)). The app's catalog, daily prices and reference images all come from TCGCSV, a one-person mirror of TCGplayer's data with no stated license ([TCGCSV](https://tcgcsv.com/)), and the public card page says so ([pages.ts](../card-db-mvp/src/render/pages.ts#L492)). eBay has owned TCGplayer since 2022 ([tcgapi.dev](https://tcgapi.dev/compare/tcgplayer-api/)), and TCGplayer's affiliates must stay in good standing with both companies ([partner guidelines](https://help.tcgplayer.com/hc/en-us/articles/31411199594391-TCGplayer-Partner-Guidelines)), so a data dispute could cost the affiliate account too.

Card images carry their own risk. Pokémon declines to review requests to use its material, and Bandai's July 10, 2026 notice reserves all rights ([Pokémon support](https://support.pokemon.com/hc/en-us/articles/360000634094-Can-I-use-Pok%C3%A9mon-images-or-materials), [Bandai notice](https://en.onepiece-cardgame.com/news/02_382.html)). Three steps reduce the risk: a notice that the site isn't affiliated with either company, sellers' own photos in place of catalog art, and a registered DMCA takedown agent ([Copyright Office](https://www.copyright.gov/dmca-directory/)). These data and image questions are decisions for a lawyer, not an engineer.

Charging money brings its own rules. Stripe expects posted refund and cancellation policies, contact methods and a business address ([Stripe checklist](https://docs.stripe.com/get-started/checklist/website)). Federal law requires clear terms and the customer's express consent before billing ([ROSCA](https://www.ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/statutes/restore-online-shoppers-confidence-act)). California's amended auto-renewal law, in force since July 1, 2025, adds consent records kept for three years, online cancellation, and annual reminders ([AB 2863](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/billTextClient.xhtml?bill_id=202320240AB2863)). The FTC's click-to-cancel rule was struck down in July 2025, and only an early-stage notice has followed ([Crowell & Moring](https://www.crowell.com/en/insights/client-alerts/clicking-all-the-right-boxes-ftc-moves-to-revive-click-to-cancel-rule-following-eighth-circuit-vacatur), [Federal Register](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/03/13/2026-04952/rule-concerning-the-use-of-prenotification-negative-option-plans)). California also starts taxing software subscriptions on Jan 1, 2027 ([Avalara](https://www.avalara.com/blog/en/north-america/2026/06/california-sales-tax-software-saas.html)). Today the plans page promises "cancel anytime" next to a disabled Subscribe button ([pricing.ts](../card-db-mvp/src/render/pricing.ts#L71)).

Email is the quiet blocker. At the last check, production had no email provider, so password-reset links went to the server log instead of the customer ([mailer.ts](../card-db-mvp/src/app/mailer.ts#L15), [inventory](../cardindex-feature-inventory.md)). Privacy law is lighter at this size, since the site is far below the thresholds of California's privacy law ([California Privacy Protection Agency](https://privacy.ca.gov/laws-and-regulations/monetary-thresholds-in-the-ccpa/)). Even so, accounts should be limited to people 18 and over. That matches eBay's and TCGplayer's rules and keeps the site clear of the amended federal children's-privacy rule ([COPPA rule](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/04/22/2025-05904/childrens-online-privacy-protection-rule)).

## The app answers to two names and promises more than it does

**The product has two names.** The domain and the new privacy page say **Trading Card Loader**. The logo, every browser-tab title, the name shown to search engines, the sign-in pages, the owner console, the share page and the password-reset email all say **CardIndex**, down to the email's default sender, `CardIndex <no-reply@localhost>` ([layout.ts](../card-db-mvp/src/render/layout.ts#L15), [server.ts](../card-db-mvp/src/server.ts#L1640), [mailer.ts](../card-db-mvp/src/app/mailer.ts#L15), [privacy.ts](../card-db-mvp/src/render/privacy.ts#L16)). The mismatch matters. The privacy page is the address eBay shows when a seller grants access, and a reset email from an unfamiliar brand looks like phishing. Aligning to the domain means changing one constant and a few dozen strings, and the live Card Index product is another reason to retire the internal name. The client should confirm the choice.

Beyond the brand, at least 30 concepts carry two to six names each. The sidebar says Configuration, while buttons, errors and even the privacy policy send sellers to "Settings → eBay" ([app.ts](../card-db-mvp/src/render/app.ts#L252), [ebay-sell.ts](../card-db-mvp/src/app/ebay-sell.ts#L281)). The listing words do the most damage, because they hide what the app actually does. Inventory flips to "Listed" the moment a draft exists. Two "Mark as listed" buttons change different records, a third button says "Mark live", and statuses print raw database words like "exported" and "published" ([store.ts](../card-db-mvp/src/app/store.ts#L857), [server.ts](../card-db-mvp/src/server.ts#L864), [workspace2.ts](../card-db-mvp/src/render/workspace2.ts#L491)).

A "Cards in stock" tile counts sold cards, shows different numbers on the Dashboard and on Inventory, and sits beside a summary line that counts only unsold stock ([app.ts](../card-db-mvp/src/render/app.ts#L494), [store.ts](../card-db-mvp/src/app/store.ts#L796)). Both add-cards tools are headed "Add cards", including the price-only one, which says nothing is added to inventory ([app.ts](../card-db-mvp/src/render/app.ts#L750)). One match state is called Failed, No match and Unmatched. The variant picker says "Printing" where the client says "variant", "Raw" competes with eBay's "Ungraded", and the sold-price tool has five names.

The rule for fixing this is short. Where the client's words, CardUploader's labels and eBay's terms agree, use that word, because users expect language they already know ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/match-system-real-world/)). For anything that ends up on eBay, use eBay's term ([eBay condition names](https://community.ebay.com/t5/Announcements/Revamp-your-trading-card-listings-with-updated-conditions/ba-p/33878006)). Where CardUploader's label is jargon, such as "Database" for the game, let the client choose. Some fixes change behaviour, not just labels. "Listed" only becomes true if it is set when a listing actually goes live, or when the seller marks it listed after a CSV upload.

**About 30 strings promise things the product doesn't do.** They are the most urgent group, because several are legal risks rather than cosmetic problems. Smaller slips belong in the same pass: "Unlimited" plans capped at 500 photos per batch, a "Free" label on a Pro-only page, the client's own game shown as "coming soon", and an owner statistic labelled "Pro (paid)" when nobody pays ([app.ts](../card-db-mvp/src/render/app.ts#L229), [workspace2.ts](../card-db-mvp/src/render/workspace2.ts#L208), [admin.ts](../card-db-mvp/src/render/admin.ts#L155)).

## Twelve moves, in order, answer the client's question

The client closed with a direct question: "is this something we can do?" ([15:38](file:///C:/Users/johnn/Downloads/2026-09-14%2020-47-22.mp4#t=938)). The findings above add up to a sequence. The first week produces evidence, and each later step removes a blocker, in the order it would stop a paying seller.

| When | Move | Why it can't wait |
|---|---|---|
| This week | Import the full Pokémon and One Piece catalogs on production and confirm the photo index covers them | Without it, the client's cards cannot match on the live site |

## Conclusion

The client asked for a yes or no and expected an answer about features. But the workflow is largely built in code already, so the answer turns on two things: a number nobody has measured, the match rate on the client's own scanner files, and one owner task, loading the catalog. Both can be done within about a week for almost nothing. That makes the honest reply precisely conditional: yes on matching CardUploader's workflow, and yes on scanning if the test clears roughly 95% top-1 and 99% top-5 on the client's images, with a vendor API as the fallback. The same test settles pricing. A vendor that charges per card makes a flat $7 unlimited plan lose money on a seller as active as the client, so choosing the recognition engine and choosing the plan structure are one decision, not two.

The deeper exposure is dependence on one company. The catalog and prices, the eBay listings popup, sold prices, the listing path and both affiliate income streams all run through eBay and its subsidiary TCGplayer. Each rests on a permission eBay can withhold: EPN approval, Browse licensing, a ruling on price summaries, and tolerance of mirrored TCGplayer data. The client's plan to undercut rivals with "other ways to make money" is therefore as much a licensing bet as a product one, and it should be tested with eBay and a lawyer before the price is fixed. The cheapest wins run the other way. Removing what the client never asked for, from the Mana Pool and Storefront tabs to an Automatic inventory page that promises sync, serves the client's wish for simplicity and deletes promises the app can't keep.
